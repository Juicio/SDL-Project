1 Research question and main finding

Research question: How does the prevalence of households without a vehicle relate to available transit service and employment accessibility across the three-county Omaha–Council Bluffs study region?

Main finding: Among census block groups with usable transit indicators, transit service generally appears concentrated in places where car-free households are more common. However, this overall pattern conceals 47 higher-car-free block groups with comparatively limited modeled transit access to employment. These are candidates for further investigation using current GTFS schedules, ridership, and local transit-service data, rather than confirmed service gaps.

2 Methods

2.1 Study area, data source, and preparation

We use the team’s filtered EPA Smart Location Database (SLD), version 3 (released in 2021), covering Douglas and Sarpy counties, Nebraska, and Pottawattamie County, Iowa. The selection includes some places outside Omaha and Council Bluffs municipal limits. The unit of analysis is a census block group, not an individual household.

We load the county-filtered CSV, convert key variables to numeric, treat the EPA missing/noncoverage code -99999 as unavailable (never as numeric zero), and reconstruct candidate 12-digit block-group identifiers from separately retained geographic components. The original GEOID10 and GEOID20 columns in the supplied export are truncated/rounded; reconstructed IDs must be validated against official boundaries before mapping.

# Keep the CSV at the repository root (one directory above abhijith).
paths <- c(
  "../EPA_SDL_OmahaCouncilBluffs.csv",
  "EPA_SDL_OmahaCouncilBluffs.csv",
  "../EPA_SDL_OmahaCouncilBluffs(1).csv",
  "EPA_SDL_OmahaCouncilBluffs(1).csv"
)
available <- paths[file.exists(paths)]
if (length(available) == 0) {
  stop("EPA_SDL_OmahaCouncilBluffs.csv not found. Place it in the repository root.")
}

d <- read.csv(available[1], stringsAsFactors = FALSE,
              colClasses = c(STATEFP = "character", COUNTYFP = "character",
                             TRACTCE = "character", BLKGRPCE = "character",
                             GEOID10 = "character", GEOID20 = "character"))
stopifnot(nrow(d) > 0, all(c("Pct_AO0", "D4A", "D4C", "D4D", "D5BR", "D5AR", "HH", "AutoOwn0") %in% names(d)))

num_vars <- c("Pct_AO0", "D4A", "D4C", "D4D", "D5BR", "D5AR", "HH", "AutoOwn0")
for (v in num_vars) {
  d[[v]] <- as.numeric(d[[v]])
  d[[v]][d[[v]] == -99999] <- NA_real_
}

# Uploaded CSV has truncated/rounded GEOID10 and GEOID20 fields.
# Reconstruct *candidate* 12-character block group IDs from intact components.
d$block_group_id_reconstructed <- paste0(
  sprintf("%02d", as.integer(d$STATEFP)),
  sprintf("%03d", as.integer(d$COUNTYFP)),
  sprintf("%06d", as.integer(d$TRACTCE)),
  sprintf("%01d", as.integer(d$BLKGRPCE))
)
stopifnot(length(unique(d$block_group_id_reconstructed)) == nrow(d))

d$county <- ifelse(d$STATEFP == "31" & as.integer(d$COUNTYFP) == 55, "Douglas",
            ifelse(d$STATEFP == "31" & as.integer(d$COUNTYFP) == 153, "Sarpy",
            ifelse(d$STATEFP == "19" & as.integer(d$COUNTYFP) == 155, "Pottawattamie", NA)))
stopifnot(!anyNA(d$county))
d$carfree_pct <- 100 * d$Pct_AO0
threshold <- as.numeric(quantile(d$Pct_AO0, probs = 0.75, na.rm = TRUE))
d$higher_carfree <- !is.na(d$Pct_AO0) & d$Pct_AO0 >= threshold
jobs_median <- median(d$D5BR, na.rm = TRUE)
d$relative_gap_flag <- d$higher_carfree & !is.na(d$D5BR) & d$D5BR < jobs_median

2.2 Measures and interpretation

EPA field What is measured How it is interpreted
Pct_AO0 Share of households with no vehicle A 0–1 proportion; multiplied by 100 to display percent
D4A Network walking distance to the nearest qualifying transit stop (meters) Lower means closer stop proximity among observed records
D4C Aggregate afternoon peak-period transit-service frequency Higher means more reported service frequency
D4D Transit service-frequency density per square mile Higher means denser reported service
D5BR Time-decay-weighted employment accessibility by public transit, under EPA’s modeled travel assumptions Higher means greater modeled transit job accessibility; not a count of every job directly reachable
D5AR Time-decay-weighted employment accessibility by automobile Higher means greater modeled access by auto, under a different mode/travel-time model

Definitions and source conventions follow the EPA Smart Location Database documentation linked in Sources below. The automobile and transit job-access measures should not be divided to obtain a literal percentage of the same jobs accessible within identical door-to-door trip times.

2.3 Missing-value handling and analytic sample

A -99999 value is treated as unavailable for that indicator. For some variables, it may reflect distance-threshold rules, incomplete GTFS-based transit coverage, or other noncoverage conditions. It is not evidence of zero transit service or zero accessible jobs. Each correlation, median, and visualization uses only valid observations for the indicator involved; denominators may therefore differ.

In particular, D5BR is measured for just 362 of 727 block groups, including only 1 of 93 Pottawattamie block groups. We do not use missing Iowa-side values as zeros or attempt a direct Omaha–Council Bluffs transit-access comparison. Coverage is reported as a result before any substantive interpretation.

2.4 Associations and group comparison

We calculate Spearman rank correlations of zero-vehicle household share (Pct_AO0) with each transit or employment-accessibility measure. This describes the direction of a monotonic association among observed block groups, without assuming linearity or establishing causation.

We compare the observed indicator medians between (a) block groups at or above the 75th percentile of the full-sample car-free household share and (b) remaining block groups. The higher-car-free cutoff is calculated once from all 727 study-area block groups: 8.68% (the upper quartile). This is a descriptive grouping, not a policy benchmark.

2.5 Relative-gap screening definition

A block group is a relative transit-employment-access gap candidate only if both of these conditions apply:

  1. Its car-free share is at or above 8.68%, the full-sample upper quartile; and
  2. Its observed D5BR score is below 9,975, the median of available transit-job scores in the study file.

Block groups with missing D5BR are not screened or flagged as low-access. The resulting flag is a project-defined, dataset-relative prioritization device, not an official transit-service or planning standard; it does not establish service insufficiency or identify a formal transit desert.

Planner use. Before choosing investment locations or comparing jurisdictions, obtain current, consistent GTFS, service-coverage and ridership information for both sides of the metro area, and examine whether omitted block groups are systematically different from observed ones.

indicators <- c("D4A", "D4C", "D4D", "D5BR", "D5AR")
comparison <- do.call(rbind, lapply(indicators, function(var) {
  valid <- !is.na(d[[var]]) & !is.na(d$Pct_AO0)

  )
}))

Among measured block groups, car-free share is positively associated with transit-job accessibility (Spearman ρ = 0.257), positively associated with peak-service frequency (ρ = 0.334), and negatively associated with distance to the nearest qualifying stop (ρ = -0.264; shorter distances are more favorable). 3.3 Figure 2 — Car-free share versus transit access to jobs

What the figure shows. The overall positive association between car-free share and modeled transit-job accessibility coexists with observations in the lower-right region of the plot: higher-car-free block groups with below-median measured job access. The dashed vertical and horizontal lines show the two relative screening thresholds; the vertical axis uses a logarithmic scale.

Why this matters. A favorable regional correlation can hide pockets of potential need. Transit proximity or service concentrated in car-free neighborhoods at a regional scale does not guarantee that every such neighborhood can reach many jobs by transit.

Planner use. Use the flagged observations as an initial shortlist for investigating job destinations, transit route connections, frequency, wait times and transfer burden—then verify with contemporary route and ridership information.

3.4 Figure 3 — Car-free share versus distance to transit stops

What the figure shows. Among block groups with an observed D4A value, higher car-free shares generally correspond to shorter walking distances to a qualifying stop (negative Spearman association). Individual block groups vary substantially around that trend.

Why this matters. Access to a nearby stop is one part of mobility, but it does not measure how often vehicles arrive or which jobs they reach.

Planner use. Compare distance-based findings with actual peak and off-peak timetables, safe walking routes, ADA accessibility and bus-stop conditions when prioritizing local improvements.

3.5 Figure 4 — Differences and variation in transit-based employment access

What the figure shows. The measured higher-car-free group has a median 29,142 weighted transit-accessible jobs, compared with 6,031 for the other measured group. Both groups nonetheless show considerable variation. The boxplot suppresses outlier markers for readability but does not exclude those observations from reported statistics.

Why this matters. Group averages or medians can obscure within-group differences. The most useful planning targets may be specific places rather than an entire county or group.

Planner use. Combine the distribution and the candidate list with local factors such as employment centers, commuting times, equity goals, and neighborhood engagement instead of allocating resources based on one group median.

3.6 Figure 5 — Modeled access to employment: transit and auto

What the figure shows. The plotted block groups have observed values for both modeled automobile (D5AR) and public-transit (D5BR) job-access indicators. The measures vary across neighborhoods and are calculated under different travel-mode and time-decay assumptions.

Why this matters. This is a useful comparative view of mode-specific accessibility, but it is not a literal share of identical jobs accessible by transit versus car. Treating the plotted values as interchangeable would misstate the results.

Planner use. Look for places where transit accessibility merits closer evaluation alongside auto access; use common-origin, current-door-to-door travel-time analyses to make any formal mode-comparison or investment decision.

3.7 Relative-gap screening: 47 candidates

The two-condition screen identifies 47 block groups among 131 higher-car-free block groups with observed transit-job data (35.9% of that observed subset). This means potential relative need, not proven service inadequacy. The ten candidates with the lowest observed D5BR are listed below; the complete file is results/relative_gap_candidates.csv.

4 Discussion, limitations, and team integration

Core interpretation. Transit service generally appears concentrated in neighborhoods where car-free households are more common among observed records, but this pattern hides 47 higher-car-free block groups with comparatively limited modeled transit access to employment. These locations are candidates for further study, not confirmed transit deserts.

  • Uneven and incomplete coverage: D5BR is observed for only 362 of 727 block groups; only 1 of 93 Pottawattamie observations has a usable D5BR value. Missing Iowa-side values must not be interpreted as zero service, and no direct Omaha–Council Bluffs D5BR comparison is warranted.
  • Historical scope: EPA SLD v3 is a 2021 release based on historical transit, demographic and employment inputs; findings are not a measure of current (2026) transit conditions.
  • Modeled versus actual experience: D5BR and D5AR are travel-model measures with different assumptions, not observed commuting outcomes. Service frequency does not capture reliability, fares, transfer quality or traveler needs.
  • Geography and ID quality: The three-county area extends beyond city limits. The supplied geographic-ID fields are damaged; reconstructed candidate IDs need external confirmation before geospatial mapping or geographic policy action.
  • Descriptive—not causal: Block-group correlations may reflect land use, housing cost, residential sorting, density and many other factors; the analysis does not establish that transit provision causes car ownership patterns.

Connections to team sections: Compare the 47 candidate flags with Adithya’s neighborhood archetypes and Juicio’s vehicle-ownership prediction to enrich interpretation. Noah’s Omaha/Council Bluffs or Nebraska/Iowa comparison should explicitly report the coverage imbalance and avoid inferring Iowa transit performance from unavailable GTFS-based scores.
